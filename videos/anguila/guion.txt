I just whistled down the well. In Wrenmoor, that's the one thing you never do. One whistle, and the thing under the well comes up. And it takes whatever whistled. Behind me, six houses are on fire. Our town sits on a rock in the middle of a cold lake. The well in the square goes straight down into the lake. We pull our water up on a rope, slow and quiet. The sign on the well says, no whistling. Not even softly. Our kettles have no whistles. Mayor Quill pulls them out with pliers. We even shoo the songbirds off our roofs. Babies here learn to hum before they learn to talk. We stuff our chimneys with rags, so the wind can't whistle either. Old Garrow says the lake was here before the town. And so was it. He is the only one alive who has seen it. Fifty years ago, the tinker's new kettle whistled on the stove. The well rope went tight. The water in the well rose up, black and slow. Then a head came out, wider than the well. An eel, gray as river stone, with white eyes like boiled eggs. It kept coming. Its body wrapped the square three times. It found the kettle. It took the whole kitchen with it. Then it slid back down, and the water went flat. The tinker never cooked again. He lives across the lake now. My little sister Wren learned to whistle last week. She practices in the barn, where nobody can hear. I told her to stop. She said, it's only a little one. Then, this morning, the bakery caught fire. The wind pushed the flames from roof to roof. We made a line to the well, bucket by bucket. It was too slow. Six houses were burning. Garrow grabbed my arm. He said, it lives in water. It's always wet. I looked at the well. I looked at the fire. Wren looked at me. She already knew. I leaned over the well, and I whistled. Loud. As loud as I could. The rope went tight. The water rose, black and slow. The eel came up, and up, and up. Everybody ran. I didn't. It slid over the bakery and wrapped around the roof. The fire hissed out under its cold, wet skin. Then the next house. Then the next. Six houses. Six coils. The whole square turned to steam. Then it turned to me. Its white eye was bigger than our door. I waited for it to take me. It didn't. It sank back down the well, slow and quiet. Now there's a new line on the sign. Whistle if it burns. My whole life, nobody whistled in Wrenmoor. Today, I whistled, and it saved us.
