Nobody blows the horn in Dustmere. Whoever blows it calls Onetusk down from the ridge. This morning, I blew it. The horn sits on our tower, long as three houses, white as bone. We don't blow it for weddings. We tap spoons on cups. We don't blow it for babies. We just whisper their names. School starts when Miss Ode waves a red flag. Even our roosters are quiet. We ate the loud ones. And up on the ridge, Onetusk stands and watches. It is taller than our tower. Its legs are like four mesas. It has one tusk. Nobody asks where the other one went. It has not moved in sixty years. My little sister Bee is six. She hears everything. Listen, she says. Beetles under the floor. Rain, two days away. Listen, she says, and points at the ridge. It's humming. Nobody else hears it. Sixty years ago, a boy named Dez blew the horn to warn us of a fire. One note. Onetusk walked down the ridge in eleven steps. It flattened nine houses, looking for the sound. Then it stood over the tower all night, and left at dawn. Its footprints are our ponds now. Eleven of them. We painted one word on the tower. Never. This morning, a sandstorm swallowed the valley. The sky went orange. I couldn't see my own hands. And Bee was outside, chasing our goat. I screamed her name. The wind ate it. Forty neighbors screamed. The wind ate that too. Only one thing in Dustmere is louder than a storm. I climbed the tower. Ninety-two steps. I put my mouth on the horn. I blew until my ribs hurt. One note, rolling over the dunes. On the ridge, Onetusk turned its head. One step. The cups jumped off our shelves. Five steps. The ponds splashed out. Eleven steps, and its shadow covered the square. Everybody ran. I stayed. I kept blowing. Its trunk wrapped around the tower. The stone cracked. Then I saw its face, right next to mine. A broken stump, where the other tusk should be. The same curve. The same cracks. The same white. We never built this horn. We cut it off. I pulled the pins. The horn slid off the tower. Onetusk caught it, and held it against its face. It closed its eyes. The humming stopped. And down by its foot, something small let go of its leg. Bee. Covered in sand. Holding the goat. I followed the humming, she said. It walked back up the ridge, carrying its tusk. My whole life, nobody blew the horn. It was never our horn. Listen, Bee said. And for the first time, I heard it too.
